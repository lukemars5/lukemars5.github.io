The Battle of Britain

This game was a project in my first year of college. I developed the game using Unity and C#. The game features endless waves of enemy planes that fly towards the player. The player must shoot down the planes to earn points and beat their high-score, which are both displayed when the player loses. The player must dodge the enemy planes and also destroy them before they fly past the player, if ten planes manage to fly past the player then the player loses.

The planes have varying levels of speed and health. The big bombers are very slow and easy to hit, but are very tanky and can survive a lot of hits. The small propeller plane is fast and hard to hit but can be destroyed quickly. The jet fighter is a mix of both attributes, they are fast and hard to hit but can also take a few hits before being destroyed.

Project Wentworth

This game was a group project in my second year of college. We developed the game using Unreal Engine 5 and Blueprints. Due to time constraints, the game had to be cut short and function as a demo/vertical slice. The demo is a first-person shooter where the player must navigate through a facility and escape from the mutants roaming the area. The demo doesn't feature working AI, so they just wander to a random location every so often.

I was responsible for developing the weapon system and the player character. In the full game, there would've been multiple weapons with each weapon class having a unique ability. In the demo, there is only an SMG which has a rapid-fire ability which can be activated with L-Alt for a short time. Due to the college course not being a programming-focused course, I also helped out with some 3D art, and I modelled the weapons and some of the props in the game. Unfortunately, due to not starting my C++ learning until after I finished the project, the game was developed exclusively with Blueprints, but this project – and previous Unreal Engine projects – did give me a good understanding of Unreal Engine, its capabilites and the overall workflow.
